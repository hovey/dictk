Continuum Mechanics

This section summarizes the kinematics of general (finite) motion, the motion map, the deformation gradient and its Jacobian, the family of finite-strain measures and their linearizations, and the polar and spectral decompositions. Lower case indices (i,j,k) denote components in the current configuration and upper case indices (I,J,K) denote components in the reference configuration.

Motion

Let the arbitrary time interval be defined as T:=[t0​,tf​], from initial to final time, inclusive.1 Let the motion, a one-parameter family of configurations, φ:B×T↦R3, map the material particle X (the reference configuration) into the current configuration x,

x=φ(X,t)=φt​(X).

A motion φ evaluated at a particular time t∈T is referred to as a current configuration or placement. For any placement at time t, there is a displacement field u:B×T↦R3,

u(X,t):=φ(X,t)−X⟺ui​:=φi​−δiJ​XJ​.

Thus, the current configuration φ is simply a function of the original placement X, plus a displacement u, which is a function of placement X and time t,

φ(X,t)=X+u(X,t)⟺φi​=δiJ​XJ​+ui​.

The initial condition u0​ is found from the initial placement φ0​ and the reference configuration X,

u0​(X)=u(X,0)=φ(X,0)−X.

Deformation Gradient

To each configuration φ, we define a deformation gradient F:B×T↦M+3​,

F:=Gradφ(X,t)⟺FiJ​:=∂XJ​∂φi​​.

Real, square matrices of dimension three with positive determinant are denoted M+3​. Gradient operations with and without a subscript "0" are gradients taken in the reference and current configurations, respectively:

Grad(∙)grad(⋅)​=∂XJ​∂(∙)​(reference configuration gradient),=∂xj​∂(⋅)​(current configuration gradient).​

Alternative notations are GRAD(∙) and grad(⋅), respectively.

Jacobian of the Deformation Gradient

The Jacobian of the deformation gradient,

J:=detF,

describes the (generally non-uniform) volumetric expansion or contraction of the motion from the reference configuration X. All configurations must be admissible in the sense that the Jacobian of the deformation must be positive (J>0). This requirement keeps the deformations from mapping the body to a single, infinitesimally small point (J=0) or turning the body inside-out (J<0).

Isochoric motions preserve the body's total volume. A Jacobian of unity (J=1) describes an isochoric motion. The table below describes the categories of motions (expansion, volume-preserving, contraction, and inadmissible) by Jacobian measure.

Four important isochoric deformations are (1) pure translation, (2) pure rotation, (3) isochoric stretch, and (4) isochoric shear.

Displacement Gradient

From the displacement field defined above, the relationship between the displacement gradient and the deformation gradient is given by

Gradu=Gradφ−I=F−I⟺∂XJ​∂ui​​=∂XJ​∂φi​​−δiJ​.

Right Cauchy-Green Deformation

The right Cauchy-Green deformation arises from the inner product of two differential fiber elements in the reference configuration, dX1​ and dX2​, mapped by the deformation gradient F to obtain the inner product of the same differential fibers in the current configuration, dx1​ and dx2​,

dx1​⋅dx2​=dx1⊤​dx2​=(FdX1​)⊤FdX2​=dX1⊤​F⊤FdX2​=dX1⊤​CdX2​,

where

C:=F⊤F⟺CIJ​:=FIi​FiJ​.

The right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor C: (1) is defined in the reference configuration, (2) is symmetric and positive-definite, (3) gets its name from the location of the deformation gradient F in the definition, which is to the right, (4) is a metric that maps fiber lengths from the reference configuration to the current configuration, and (5) is second-order in reference displacement gradients, as shown below:

C=(Gradφ)⊤Gradφ​=(I+Gradu)⊤(I+Gradu),=I+1st orderGradu+(Gradu)⊤​​+2nd order(Gradu)⊤Gradu​​.​

This result can be expected since, by definition, C is second-order in the deformation gradient Gradφ, and the relationship between the deformation gradient Gradφ and the displacement gradient Gradu is linear.

Left Cauchy-Green Deformation

The left Cauchy-Green deformation arises from similar multiplication as with the right Cauchy-Green deformation, but with the stretching going in reverse, from the current configuration back to the reference configuration,

dX1​⋅dX2​=dX1⊤​dX2​=(F−1dx1​)⊤F−1dx2​=dx1⊤​F−⊤F−1dx2​=dx1⊤​b−1dx2​,

where

b:=FF⊤⟺bij​:=FiJ​FJj​.

The left Cauchy-Green deformation tensor b: (1) is defined in the current configuration, (2) is symmetric and positive-definite, (3) gets its name from the location of the deformation gradient F in the definition, which is to the left, (4) is a metric whose inverse maps fiber lengths from the current configuration to the reference configuration, and (5) is second-order in current displacement gradients.

Green-Lagrange Strain

The Green-Lagrange strain tensor,

E:=21​(C−I)⟺EIJ​:=21​(CIJ​−δIJ​),

is closely related to the right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor and is often used in defining constitutive law relationships because the measure, when linearized about the reference configuration, coincides with the small strain tensor of linear deformation elasticity, denoted ϵ and defined in the Infinitesimal Strain section. This relationship can be seen as follows:

2E2ELIN​​=I+Gradu+(Gradu)⊤+(Gradu)⊤Gradu−I,=Gradu+(Gradu)⊤,​

where the higher-order (quadratic) term in the first line is set to zero to achieve the linearized second line.

Euler-Almansi Strain

The Euler-Almansi strain tensor,

e:=21​(I−b−1)⟺eij​:=21​(δij​−(bij​)−1),

can likewise be used to approximate the small strain tensor ϵ by combining the definitions of the left Cauchy-Green deformation and the deformation gradient as follows:

2e2eLIN​​=I−[Gradφ(Gradφ)⊤]−1=δij​−[∂XJ​∂φi​​∂XJ​∂φj​​]−1=δij​−[∂φi​∂XJ​​∂φj​∂XJ​​]=I−[(I−gradu)(I−gradu)⊤]=gradu+gradu⊤−gradugradu⊤,=gradu+(gradu)⊤,​

where the higher-order (quadratic) term is set to zero to achieve the linearized final line.

Small Strain

When displacement gradients are small in the reference configuration,

Gradu≪1,

or in the current configuration,

gradu≪1,

respectively, the nonlinear gradient terms are negligible and the finite strain theory simplifies to small strain theory, which occurs when finite strain measures are linearized to obtain ELIN​ and eLIN​ in the previous sections.

Note that we have restricted the gradients of displacement, and not the displacement u itself. Thus, displacements between the reference and current configurations can be large (finite), but the gradients of the displacement, either in the reference or current configuration, are small.

The Strain Tensors and Finite Rotations section will demonstrate that the small strain tensors are not suitable to describe motion that contains finite rotation. This makes sense because, in finite rotation, gradients of displacement are large, not small. To adequately describe motion that includes finite rotation, a fully nonlinear strain measure, such as the Seth-Hill strain family, must be used.

Infinitesimal Strain

If we further restrict the small strain theory such that the displacement is small compared to unity,

u≪1,

the infinitesimal strain theory is obtained, which has no distinction between Lagrangian and Eulerian strain tensors.

In this case, the two small strain tensors, ELIN​ and eLIN​, converge to a single definition of strain, called the infinitesimal strain tensor ϵ, defined as

ϵ:=21​(gradu+(gradu)⊤).

Note that the Grad(∙) notation has been dropped since the distinction between the reference and current configurations is nonexistent. Also, note that the factor of 21​ appears because it then follows that the infinitesimal strain is simply the symmetric part of the displacement gradient,

ϵ=sym(gradu).

Finally, note that the finite Lagrangian and Eulerian strain tensors were defined with the factor of 21​ so that their expressions, once linearized and subject to a small displacement assumption, simplify to exactly the infinitesimal strain tensor ϵ.

Strain Tensors and Finite Rotations

Because it takes on nonzero values under finite rotation, the linearized strain tensor should not be used for geometrically nonlinear analysis. These nonzero values are completely artificial and strictly an artifact of using a linear strain definition with geometrically nonlinear motions. This result is shown as follows.

Let R=R(t) be a two-dimensional, rigid body rotation parameterized by time t and scaled by constant ω=2π radians per second. Then, the motion φ of a body can be written as

φ(X,t)=R(t)X⟺{x1​x2​​}=[cosωt−sinωt​sinωtcosωt​]{X1​X2​​}.

Then the deformation gradient F=F(t) is a function of time alone,

F(t)⟺FiJ​=[F11​F21​​F12​F22​​]=[cosωt−sinωt​sinωtcosωt​].

The linearized strain tensor is found to be

2ELIN​​=Gradu+(Gradu)⊤,=F−I+(F−I)⊤,=FiJ​−δiJ​+FJi​−δJi​,=[cosωt−sinωt​sinωtcosωt​]+[cosωtsinωt​−sinωtcosωt​]−[20​02​],=2(cosωt−1)I.​

Now, for small angles, ωt≈0, which is for small deviations x1​≈X1​, x2​≈X2​, then ELIN​=0 for rigid body rotations. However, for arbitrary finite angles, ωt=0, and the linearized strain tensor ELIN​ reports nonzero strain for rigid body rotations, which is nonsensical.

A correct strain tensor will report zero strain for rigid body rotations. One such strain tensor is the fully nonlinear Green-Lagrange strain tensor. This result is shown as follows:

2E​=F⊤F−I=[cosωtsinωt​−sinωtcosωt​][cosωt−sinωt​sinωtcosωt​]−[10​01​]=0.​

Polar Decomposition

Given the rotation tensor R, the material stretch tensor U, and the spatial stretch tensor v, the deformation gradient F has the multiplicative decomposition,

F=RU=vR⟺FiJ​=RiK​UKJ​=vik​RkJ​.

Here we have a slight abuse of notation, where intermediate configurations that have stretched but not yet rotated are denoted with capital letter indices. Thus the "K" subscript in RiK​UKJ​ is an intermediate stretched but non-rotated configuration.

The stretch tensors U and v are both symmetric and positive definite. The rotation tensor R is non-symmetric and orthogonal. The figure below shows the polar decomposition about a material point X and fibers dX in its vicinity mapped to the spatial point x with the same fibers mapped to dx.

Principal Stretches and Axes

The stretch tensors U and v have the same eigenvalues, (λ1​,λ2​,λ3​), called principal stretches. For non-trivial rotations, i.e. R=I, U and v have unique eigenvectors, called principal stretch directions. The principal stretch directions of U are {N1​,N2​,N3​}. The principal stretch directions of v are {n1​,n2​,n3​}. The two sets of eigenvectors are related through rotation R,

n1​=RN1​,n2​=RN2​,n3​=RN3​;

or generally,

nα​=RNα​for α=1,2,3.

Spectral Representation

The deformation gradient, its polar decomposition, and the Cauchy-Green deformations have spectral decompositions in terms of the principal stretches and stretch directions,

FRC​=α=1∑3​λα​nα​⊗Nα​,=α=1∑3​nα​⊗Nα​,=α=1∑3​λα2​Nα​⊗Nα​,​Uvb​=α=1∑3​λα​Nα​⊗Nα​,=α=1∑3​λα​nα​⊗nα​,=α=1∑3​λα2​nα​⊗nα​.​

The Green-Lagrange strain tensor E and the Euler-Almansi strain tensor e, in principal stretches and stretch directions, are

E=α=1∑3​21​(λα2​−1)Nα​⊗Nα​,e=α=1∑3​21​(1−λα−2​)nα​⊗nα​.

The generalization of the Seth-Hill material strain tensor E(m) and spatial strain tensor e(m), in principal stretches and stretch directions, are

E(m)=α=1∑3​m1​(λαm​−1)Nα​⊗Nα​,e(m)=α=1∑3​m1​(1−λα−m​)nα​⊗nα​,

and the relationship between the two strain tensors is given through a rotation R transformation,

e(−m)=RE(m)R⊤.

In the case when m→∞, the material and spatial logarithmic strain tensors, also known as the Hencky material and spatial strain tensors, H and h, are obtained as5

H=lnU=E(0)h=lnv=e(0)​=α=1∑3​lnλα​Nα​⊗Nα​, and=α=1∑3​lnλα​nα​⊗nα​.​

Two concrete illustrations follow: Rigid Body Motion works through pure translation as the simplest possible deformation, and Simple Shear works through an isochoric shear in closed form, computing F, C, E, b, and e explicitly.
