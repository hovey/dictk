dictk User Guide

Strain Window

Kernel Warping cut the E11​ standard deviation of the 2862-point grid to 1161 microstrain. VIC-2D's own measurement of the same stretch has a standard deviation of 1385 microstrain. That page also ran an exploratory test. A 15x15-point least-squares window cut the 1161 microstrain to 39 microstrain.

This page derives that window.

  1. Every tracked point has a tracking error.
  2. A strain needs a derivative, and a derivative amplifies those errors.
  3. A window fits one straight line to many points. The line averages their errors, so the strain error drops.
  4. The line assumes the strain is uniform across the whole window.
    • With 15x15 points at 5 px spacing, the window covers 75 px. If the real strain changes inside those 75 px, the fit blurs that change into one number.
    • A larger window averages more errors and blurs more of the real field. A smaller window does the reverse.

This derivation proceeds in three steps. A row of 3 points shows the idea with 3 numbers. A 3x3 window adds the second axis. A general window with n=2m+1 points per side repeats the 3x3 steps with 1 replaced by m.

1D Example

Consider three points on the X axis centered locally at the middle point, with relative X coordinates [−h,0,h] pixels. Each of these points has tracked displacements [u−1​,u0​,u1​]. Let c be the index of the points [−1,0,1]. Figure 9.4-1 shows the three points and their displacements.

Fit a straight line through the displacements. Point c sits at X=ch pixels relative to the middle point, so the line predicts

uc​≈u^0​+∂X∂u​ch.(9.4-1)

The slope of the line is ∂u/∂X. The intercept of the line is u^0​, the fitted displacement at the middle point, where c=0. Figure 9.4-2 shows the line through the three displacements of Figure 9.4-1.

That is two unknowns, u^0​ and ∂u/∂X, for three points. Least squares picks the two unknowns that minimize the sum of the three squared residuals:

R​=c=−1∑1​(uc​−u^0​−∂X∂u​ch)2=(u−1​−u^0​+∂X∂u​h)2+(u0​−u^0​)2+(u1​−u^0​−∂X∂u​h)2.​

At the minimum, the derivative of R with respect to each unknown is zero. The derivative with respect to u^0​ is

∂u^0​∂R​=−2c∑​(uc​−u^0​−∂X∂u​ch)=0.

Rearranged, that gives the first normal equation:

u^0​c∑​1+∂X∂u​hc∑​c=c∑​uc​.

The derivative with respect to the slope ∂u/∂X is

∂(∂u/∂X)∂R​=−2c∑​ch(uc​−u^0​−∂X∂u​ch)=0.

Dividing by −2 and rearranging gives the second normal equation:

u^0​hc∑​c+∂X∂u​h2c∑​c2=hc∑​cuc​.

In matrix form, the two normal equations are

[∑c​1h∑c​c​h∑c​ch2∑c​c2​]{u^0​∂u/∂X​}={∑c​uc​h∑c​cuc​​}.

The sums are ∑c​1=3, ∑c​c=−1+0+1=0, and ∑c​c2=1+0+1=2. So the matrix is

[30​02h2​].

The zero off the diagonal decouples the two unknowns:

u^0​∂X∂u​​=3u−1​+u0​+u1​​,=2h2h(−u−1​+u1​)​=2hu1​−u−1​​.​​(9.4-2)

The slope is the central difference. The middle point gets zero weight in it.

Noise

How much does tracking error move the slope? Work it out in six steps.

Step 1: model the error. Each tracked displacement is the true displacement plus an error:

uc​=uctrue​+ec​.

The three errors e−1​, e0​, and e1​ are independent. Each has mean 0 and standard deviation σ, so each has variance σ2.

Step 2: find the error in the slope. Substitute step 1 into the slope:

∂X∂u​=2hu1​−u−1​​=true slope2hu1true​−u−1true​​​​+slope error2he1​−e−1​​​​.

The true part has no randomness. So the slope's variance is the variance of the slope error alone. The middle point's error e0​ does not appear, because the middle point has zero weight.

Step 3: recall two variance rules. For a constant α and independent errors ea​ and eb​:

Var(αea​)Var(ea​±eb​)​=α2Var(ea​),=Var(ea​)+Var(eb​).​

Variances add for a difference too, because the sign flips with α=−1 and α2=1.

Step 4: apply the first rule to each term. The slope error has two terms, e1​/2h and −e−1​/2h. Each one is an error scaled by α=±1/(2h):

Var(2he1​​)=Var(2h−e−1​​)=4h2σ2​.

Step 5: apply the second rule to add them.

Var(∂X∂u​)=4h2σ2​+4h2σ2​=2h2σ2​.

Step 6: take the square root. The standard deviation is

std(∂X∂u​)=2​hσ​=0.707hσ​.(9.4-3)

For example, take σ=0.01 px and h=5 px. The slope's standard deviation is 0.707×0.01/5=0.00141, or 1414 microstrain.

A slope's noise reads as strain noise because, at small strain, E11​≈∂u/∂X. For a uniaxial stretch, E11​=ln(1+∂u/∂X), so dE11​/d(∂u/∂X)=1/(1+∂u/∂X). At the 2% stretch on these pages, that factor is 1/1.02=0.98. So log strain noise runs 2% below slope noise. This page quotes slope noise. From Gradient to Strain gives the full chain from slopes to strain.

The slope uses only 2 of the 3 points. The 3x3 window below averages three such rows.

Q4 Baseline

Finite Element Method computes strain from Q4 elements. Every strain on the earlier pages comes from them. This section derives a Q4 element's noise. The window sections below compare against it, for two reasons.

  • Q4 is the method dictk uses now. Kernel Warping measured its 1161 microstrain spread with gauss_point_log_strains, which evaluates Q4 elements. A window must beat that to earn a place in dictk.
  • Q4 sets the unit of improvement. The 1D slope's noise scales with σ/h. The Q4 element's and the window's do too. One number for each turns every comparison into a ratio.

Gauss-Point Gradient

Take a square Q4 element with side L. Its nodes 1 to 4 sit at (ξ,η)=(−1,−1), (1,−1), (1,1), and (−1,1). At the two Gauss points with η=+1/3​, its gradient is

∂X∂u​g1,2​​=L1​[g1​(u2​−u1​)+g2​(u3​−u4​)],=21∓1/3​​.​(9.4-4)

So g1​=0.211 and g2​=0.789. The edge nearer the Gauss point gets the larger weight. The two Gauss points at η=−1/3​ swap g1​ and g2​. Figure 9.4-3 shows the weights. The script computes them with dictk.element.

Q4 Noise

The weights satisfy g1​+g2​=1 and g12​+g22​=2/3. Each node enters once, with weight ±g1​/L or ±g2​/L. The variance rules from the 1D Example's Noise give

std(∂X∂u​)=Lσ​2(g12​+g22​)​=3​2​Lσ​=1.155Lσ​.(9.4-5)

All four Gauss points give the same value, because each one uses the same four weights in a different order.

The earlier pages build each Q4 element from 4 neighboring tracked points, so L=h. The standard deviation is then 1.155σ/h. For σ=0.01 px and h=5 px, that is 2309 microstrain. The 1D Example's central difference gives 1414 microstrain at the same σ and h. The 2D Example compares the 3x3 window with both.

2D Example

The smallest square window with a center point holds 3x3 = 9 points. It is the general window with m=1.

Setup

The points sit on a grid with spacing h. Point (c,r) sits at X=ch and Y=rh pixels relative to the center point (0,0). The window fits a plane through the 9 tracked displacements:

uc,r​≈u^0​+∂X∂u​ch+∂Y∂u​rh.(9.4-6)

That is three unknowns, u^0​, ∂u/∂X, and ∂u/∂Y, for nine points. Figure 9.4-4 shows the plane through nine displacements. It extends Figure 9.4-2 from a line to a plane.

Normal Equations

Least squares minimizes the sum of the nine squared residuals:

R=c=−1∑1​r=−1∑1​(uc,r​−u^0​−∂X∂u​ch−∂Y∂u​rh)2.

As in the 1D Example, each derivative of R is zero at the minimum. Write e^c,r​ for the residual inside the parentheses. The three derivatives are

∂u^0​∂R​∂(∂u/∂X)∂R​∂(∂u/∂Y)∂R​​=−2c,r∑​e^c,r​=0,=−2c,r∑​che^c,r​=0,=−2c,r∑​rhe^c,r​=0.​

Dividing each by −2 and rearranging gives three normal equations. In matrix form, they are

​∑1h∑ch∑r​h∑ch2∑c2h2∑cr​h∑rh2∑crh2∑r2​​⎩⎨⎧​u^0​∂u/∂X∂u/∂Y​⎭⎬⎫​=⎩⎨⎧​∑uc,r​h∑cuc,r​h∑ruc,r​​⎭⎬⎫​.(9.4-7)

Each sum runs over the 9 points:

  • ∑1=9.
  • ∑c=0. Three points sit at each of c=−1, 0, and 1, so they cancel. ∑r=0 for the same reason.
  • ∑cr=0. Flipping c to −c flips the product's sign, so the points cancel in pairs.
  • ∑c2=3+0+3=6. ∑r2=6 for the same reason.

So the matrix is

​900​06h20​006h2​​.

The matrix is diagonal, so each unknown solves on its own.

Solution

Each unknown divides one right-side entry by one diagonal entry:

u^0​∂X∂u​∂Y∂u​​=91​c,r∑​uc,r​,=6h2h∑cuc,r​​=6h1​c,r∑​cuc,r​,=6h2h∑ruc,r​​=6h1​c,r∑​ruc,r​.​​(9.4-8)

Figure 9.4-5 draws the weights. In ∂u/∂X, the right column enters with +1, the left column with −1, and the middle column with 0. Group the sum by row. Each row contributes u1,r​−u−1,r​. In ∂u/∂Y, the bottom row enters with +1, the top row with −1, and the middle row with 0. Group that sum by column. Each column contributes uc,1​−uc,−1​. So

∂X∂u​∂Y∂u​​=31​r=−1∑1​2hu1,r​−u−1,r​​,=31​c=−1∑1​2huc,1​−uc,−1​​.​

  • ∂u/∂X is the mean of three central differences, one per row. The window differentiates along X and averages along Y.
  • ∂u/∂Y is the mean of three central differences, one per column. The window differentiates along Y and averages along X.

Fit for v

Every entry of the matrix in the normal equations is a sum over c, r, and h. No entry holds a displacement. So the fit for v uses the same matrix. Only the right side changes, from uc,r​ to vc,r​. The same diagonal entries give

v^0​∂X∂v​∂Y∂v​​=91​c,r∑​vc,r​,=6h1​c,r∑​cvc,r​,=6h1​c,r∑​rvc,r​.​​(9.4-9)

The weights match Figure 9.4-5. They multiply v instead of u.

Noise in the 3x3 Window

The solution writes ∂u/∂X as a weighted sum ∑wc,rX​uc,r​ with wc,rX​=c/(6h). Independent errors give

Var(∂X∂u​)=σ2c,r∑​(wc,rX​)2=σ2(6h)26​=6h2σ2​.(9.4-10)

The standard deviation is σ/(6​h)=0.408σ/h. The row of three points gave 0.707σ/h. Averaging three independent rows divides that by 3​, and 0.707/3​=0.408. For σ=0.01 px and h=5 px, the formula gives 816 microstrain. The 10,000 Monte Carlo draws give 819 microstrain.

The weights for ∂u/∂Y are r/(6h). Their squares sum to the same 6/(6h)2, so ∂u/∂Y also has standard deviation 0.408σ/h. The fit for v uses the same weights. If the errors in v have the same σ, ∂v/∂X and ∂v/∂Y have 0.408σ/h too.

General Window

A general window holds n×n points, with n=2m+1. The steps match the 3x3 window's. Each one ends by setting m=1, which recovers the 3x3 result.

Setup

Columns c and rows r each run from −m to m. The fit is the same plane:

uc,r​≈u^0​+∂X∂u​ch+∂Y∂u​rh.

That is three unknowns for n2 points. For n=15 and h=5 px, the window holds 225 points. Its extent is nh=75 px, because each point stands for a cell h wide. The two outermost points sit (n−1)h=70 px apart. Every "window spanning" figure in this book uses the extent nh.

Normal Equations

The residual sum R, its derivatives, and the matrix form match the 3x3 window's. Only the limits change: every sum now runs from −m to m. One sum appears repeatedly. Call it Sm​:

Sm​=c=−m∑m​c2=3m(m+1)(2m+1)​.(9.4-11)

The window's sums follow from it:

  • ∑1=n2.
  • ∑c=∑r=∑cr=0, by the same cancellations as the 3x3 window.
  • ∑c2=nSm​, because each of the n rows contributes Sm​. ∑r2 is the same.

So the matrix is

​n200​0h2nSm​0​00h2nSm​​​.

At m=1: n=3 and S1​=2, so the diagonal is (9,6h2,6h2).

Solution

u^0​∂X∂u​∂Y∂u​v^0​∂X∂v​∂Y∂v​wc,rX​wc,rY​​=n21​c,r∑​uc,r​,=h2nSm​h∑cuc,r​​=c,r∑​wc,rX​uc,r​,=h2nSm​h∑ruc,r​​=c,r∑​wc,rY​uc,r​,=n21​c,r∑​vc,r​,=c,r∑​wc,rX​vc,r​,=c,r∑​wc,rY​vc,r​,=hnSm​c​,=hnSm​r​.​​(9.4-12)

At m=1, the weights are wc,rX​=c/(6h) and wc,rY​=r/(6h).

The weight grows linearly with c. The outermost columns, at c=±m, carry the largest weights. A displacement mh from the center changes by mh times any change in slope, so it constrains the slope m times as tightly as a point at c=±1. The weight does not depend on r. So the estimator fits one line per row and averages the n row slopes. Figure 9.4-7 shows the weights for m=1, 2, and 3. The script also solves the normal equations directly and compares:

Noise in a General Window

Independent errors give

Var(∂X∂u​)=σ2c,r∑​(wc,rX​)2=σ2(hnSm​)2nSm​​=h2nSm​σ2​=h2n2m(m+1)3σ2​.

The standard deviation is

std(∂X∂u​)=hσ​⋅n1​m(m+1)3​​≈hn212​σ​for large m.(9.4-13)

At m=1, it is σ/(6​h). The standard deviation falls as n−2. Each row's line fit contributes n−3/2. A row has n points, and its outer points sit mh from the center, so Sm​ grows as m3. Averaging the n rows contributes the remaining n−1/2.

Uniform Weights

Every point here gets the same weight in the sum of squared residuals. For independent errors with equal variance, uniform weights give the lowest variance of any linear unbiased estimator. A tent or Gaussian weight would reduce the influence of each window's outer points. Both are candidate refinements for a later stage. Neither appears here.

Window versus Q4

Q4 Baseline derived a Q4 element's noise, 1.155σ/L. Noise in a General Window derived the window's. This section compares the two at any window size. Two points frame the comparison.

  • Q4 shows where the gain comes from. A Q4 element fits 4 coefficients to 4 displacements. The fit is exact, so it averages nothing. A 15x15 window fits 3 coefficients to 225 displacements. That redundancy does the averaging.
  • A fair comparison controls for footprint. On a grid with spacing h, a Q4 element spans h. A 15x15 window spans 14h between its outer points. That larger footprint alone accounts for 14× of the window's 74.8× lead. It also blurs the strain field. Equal Footprint holds the footprint fixed. Q4 at Equal Resolution holds the resolution fixed, which is the fairer test.

Equal Spacing

A Q4 element at L=h gives 2309 microstrain. The 3x3 window gives 816 microstrain, 2.8 times less, at the same σ=0.01 px and h=5 px. Dividing the Q4 standard deviation by the general window's standard deviation gives the ratio for any window size:

stdwindow​stdQ4​​=32nm(m+1)​​.(9.4-14)

The ratio is 2.8 at m=1 and 74.8 at m=7. It gives the gain dictk would see by replacing its Q4 elements with windows on the same grid. It does not split that gain by source.

Equal Footprint

A 15x15 window at h=5 px spans 70 px between its outer points. A Q4 element at L=h spans 5 px. To remove the footprint gain, give the Q4 element the window's footprint. Its corners then sit on the window's 4 corner points, and L=2mh. Figure 9.4-9 shows the three choices on one grid.

With L=2mh, the ratio becomes

stdwindow​stdQ4​​=3mnm(m+1)​​.(9.4-15)

The ratio is 1.41 at m=1 and 5.35 at m=7, and it approaches n/3 as m grows. The two ratios differ by exactly 2m, the ratio of the footprints. At n=15, 74.8=14×5.35. The 14 comes from the footprint. The 5.35 comes from the fit. The window fits 225 displacements by least squares. The Q4 element interpolates 4 exactly.

Counting points alone suggests 225/4​=7.5×. Two effects turn that into 5.35×. First, the window's interior points sit closer to its center. A change in slope moves a point in column c by an amount proportional to c. So each point adds information about the slope in proportion to c2. A point at c=1 adds 1/49 as much as a point at c=7. The 15 points at c=0 add none. Over one row, ∑c2=0.381nm2, so the 225 points carry as much as 85.7 points in the outer columns would. That costs the window a factor 0.381​=0.617. Second, the Q4 element weights its corners unequally, with g1​=0.211 and g2​=0.789. Equal weights of 0.5 would give 1.0σ/L instead of 1.155σ/L. That costs the Q4 element a factor 1.155. Together, 7.5×0.617×1.155=5.35.

From Gradient to Strain

Fitting u and v gives the four components of the displacement gradient at the window center:

∇0​u=[∂u/∂X∂v/∂X​∂u/∂Y∂v/∂Y​].(9.4-16)

The fit uses reference positions ch and rh, so this is the Lagrangian gradient. The rest follows Continuum Mechanics:

FUE(0)​=I+∇0​u,=F⊤F​,=lnU.​(9.4-17)

This page reports the log strain E(0), to match the figures on the pages before it. The fit is linear in the displacements. The strain is not, because U and ln are nonlinear. So the noise formulas above hold exactly for ∇0​u. For strain they hold to first order. The 1D Example's Noise showed that log strain noise runs 2% below slope noise at the 2% stretch on these pages.

Fitting the current positions x instead of u and v gives F directly as the slope. Both routes agree, because the reference positions ch and rh are exactly linear in c and r.

Bias and Resolution

A window reduces variance and adds bias. Two results size the bias.

Cubic term. Let the true field be uc,r​=61​u′′′(ch)3, with u′′′=∂3u/∂X3. The true gradient at the center is 0. The fitted slope is

∂X∂u​​fit​=h2∑c2h∑cuc,r​​=6u′′′​h2∑c2∑c4​=30u′′′h2(3m2+3m−1)​.(9.4-18)

At m=1, that is u′′′h2/6. The standard deviation falls as n−2, and this bias grows as n2. So the mean squared error, variance plus squared bias, has a minimum. The window size at that minimum depends on u′′′ and on σ.

A quadratic fit gives the same center slope as the affine fit. A quadratic term is even about the center, and the slope weight c is odd. In a symmetric window, the two are orthogonal. Only a cubic fit removes the bias above, and it raises the variance.

Frequency Response

Take u=bsin(kX), with wavenumber k=2π/period. The window's half-width is a=nh/2, half its extent. In the continuum limit, the ratio of the fitted slope to the true slope at the window center is

T(k)z​=z33(sinz−zcosz)​≈1−10z2​for z≪1,=ka=2knh​.​(9.4-19)

The small-z limit matches the cubic bias above. At m=7, the two slope errors agree within 1%.

Window Edges

Near the grid boundary, the window truncates. Every tracked point still gets a strain. A truncated window loses the cancellations, so ∑c and ∑r no longer vanish. The matrix is no longer diagonal, and the fit solves the full 3×3 normal equations. Figure 9.4-13 shows interior, edge, and corner windows for m=3.

Truncation costs precision in two ways. Take the X gradient of a window that lost its −X half, with m+1 columns left.

Variance. The slope variance is σ2 divided by h2∑(c−cˉ)2, where cˉ is the mean column of the kept points. That sum falls from h2nSm​ to h2nm(m+1)(m+2)/12. The standard deviation rises by the factor

m+24(2m+1)​​.(9.4-21)

At m=7, the factor is 2.58. At a corner, the window also keeps only m+1 rows. The factor becomes

(m+1)(m+2)4(2m+1)2​​,(9.4-22)

which is 3.54 at m=7. At m=3, the factors are 2.37 and 3.13, as Figure 9.4-13 shows.

In units of σ/h, a 15x15 window's standard deviation is 0.0154 in the interior, 0.0398 on an edge, and 0.0545 at a corner. The Q4 element gives 1.155. The edge value is 29 times lower than that. The corner value is 21 times lower.

Location. Take a quadratic field, uc,r​=21​u′′(ch)2, with u′′=∂2u/∂X2. An affine fit returns the true gradient at the centroid of the kept points, not at the window center. This holds for any point set symmetric about its own centroid, and a truncated window is one. The centroid sits mh/2 from the window center. At m=7 and h=5 px, that shift is 17.5 px. The gradient error at an edge point is u′′⋅mh/2.

Uniform stretch has u′′=0, so this bias vanishes there. A field with curvature near an edge does show it.

Overlapping Windows

A window can step by 1 point, so every tracked point gets a strain. Or it can step by n points, so no two windows share a point.

At a step of 1, neighboring windows share n2−n of their n2 points. For n=15, that is 210 of 225. Their errors correlate. The standard deviation across the field then describes the spread of the strain field. It does not describe the uncertainty of independent measurements. Every spread quoted on this page will name its step.
